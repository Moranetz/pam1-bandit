The model

400 Kenyon cells (FlyWire counts about 2,600 per hemisphere). Each lever's cue lights a sparse 5% of them. Two output pathways, approach and avoid, read those cells through synapses that only ever weaken: dopamine arriving while a Kenyon cell is active depresses that synapse. That is the one plasticity the fly mushroom body is known to use.

Reward neurons (PAM) weaken the avoid synapses. Punishment and omission neurons (PPL1) weaken the approach synapses. The output neurons feed back onto the dopamine neurons, so dopamine fires on surprise: PAM = max(0, r − V), PPL1 = max(0, V − r), following Bennett et al. and Springer & Nawrot.

Drive above 0.35 also writes a consolidated copy of the synapse that recovers 25× slower, a stand-in for long-term memory. Opto adds dopamine that no prediction can cancel: PAM = D + max(0, r − V), which is Redish's 2004 addiction model moved into fly circuitry.

What it is not
  • It isn't the 139k-neuron Eon/FlyWire emulation. That model's synapses are frozen, so it can't learn a lever at all. This is the learning circuit that would have to be added to it.
  • The weights here are hand-set, not taken from the FlyWire connectome. The parameters are plausible, not fitted.
  • Once a memory consolidates, this fly never fully quits. Its approach synapses recover faster than disappointment pushes them down, so it drifts into a slow pull cycle on a dead machine. Extinguished flies relapse in real life too, but the size of that cycle here comes from the recovery rate I picked.
  • Nobody has shown a near-miss effect in flies. Here a near-miss is a cue that shares 60% of its Kenyon cells with the win cue, and the table shows what that sharing does.
